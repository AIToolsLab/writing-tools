Generate a realistic corporate communication scenario for a writing task in an experiment. The participant will play a novice employee who must gather information from a simulated colleague through chat and then write a professional email to an external stakeholder.

The scenario should test the participant's ability to recognize and analyze a rhetorical situation rather than simply retrieve the information needed to complete a straightforward communication task.

## Rhetors

- The participant is the writer.
- The writer is an apprentice, intern, or entry-level worker in the organization.
- The writer is representing the organization in the communication, and it is important for the organization to maintain favorable relationships with its stakeholders and a positive public reputation.
- How successfully the writer handles the communication directly affects how they are perceived within the organization.
- The writer has novice-level experience handling corporate communications.
- The writer is not authorized to make significant business, financial, or logistical decisions without approval from an experienced colleague.

## Audiences

- There must be one explicitly identified primary recipient.
- The recipient must be a customer, client, partner, vendor, or other external stakeholder of the organization.
- The recipient must have important and specific stakes in the situation.
- Successfully communicating with the recipient should require considering their knowledge, values, expectations, likely reactions, concerns, and questions.
- The writer has never interacted with the recipient before, although the organization may have an established relationship or prior communications with them.
- The scenario may contain secondary audiences who could reasonably see or be affected by the email.

## Exigences

The scenario must contain two distinct levels of exigence.

### 1. Apparent exigence

There must be an immediately recognizable problem that explains why the email needs to be written.

This should be apparent from the colleague's initial chat messages and should provide enough information for a participant to write a superficially reasonable email without further investigation.

### 2. Deeper exigence

There must also be a more consequential rhetorical problem that is NOT initially disclosed.

The deeper problem should become apparent as the participant asks the colleague semantically relevant questions or reasonable follow-ups about the broader circumstances and interprets the information provided. The participant should not need to guess exact questions or keywords.

The deeper exigence may arise from factors such as:

- prior interactions with the recipient;
- promises or expectations established by previous communications;
- previous failures or conflicts;
- the recipient's particular responsibilities or pressures;
- consequences of the problem that are not initially obvious;
- organizational reputation or credibility;
- an important upcoming event or deadline;
- competing stakeholder interests;
- potential future business or relationships;
- social, cultural, economic, or institutional circumstances.

The deeper exigence must materially change how a rhetorically sophisticated writer would approach the email. It should affect matters such as what information to include, what to emphasize, what tone to use, what concerns to anticipate, what resolution to propose, or what promises to avoid.

Do not make the deeper exigence merely an additional logistical fact. It should reveal something important about the rhetorical stakes of the communication.

## Constraints

- The required communication medium is email, so professional email conventions apply.
- Include at least one meaningful constraint beyond the communication medium.
- Constraints may arise from:
  - previous communications or promises;
  - organizational policies;
  - limits on the writer's authority;
  - schedules or deadlines;
  - contractual expectations;
  - reputational concerns;
  - social or cultural expectations;
  - economic circumstances;
  - competing stakeholder interests.
- Constraints should create rhetorical tradeoffs rather than make the appropriate response obvious.

## Discoverability of the Deeper Rhetorical Situation

The deeper exigence must be hidden but discoverable.

The participant should not be expected to infer, without any evidence, that an unrelated hidden circumstance exists. The initial situation must contain one or more natural rhetorical breadcrumbs or cues indicating that there is relevant context beyond the apparent problem.

These cues should signal the category of information worth investigating without revealing the underlying facts or the deeper exigence itself. For example:

- "There's some history with this client."
- "They were pretty concerned about this the last time we talked."
- "Timing matters more than usual for them."
- "This is a little sensitive because of what happened last time."
- "Check with me before promising anything."

A cue may indicate that prior communications, audience expectations, timing, organizational history, authority, or other circumstances matter. It should sound like a natural workplace comment, not an explicit checklist of questions or an explanation of the rhetorical problem.

The participant should still need to ask an appropriate follow-up question to discover the relevant information. For example, "There's some history with this client" could lead to "What happened with them before?" The answer might reveal a previous delayed shipment and the assurance made afterward, leaving the participant to interpret their significance for the current email.

At least one cue pointing toward each essential component of the deeper exigence must be reasonably available through the initial chat or the colleague's responses to ordinary, relevant questions. If a cue appears later, there must be a natural path to it from information already available; it must not depend on first guessing the hidden fact it is meant to help uncover.

Use the same initial cues and disclosure rules for participants with and without AI assistance. The scenario should reward recognizing and investigating rhetorically salient cues, not guessing that hidden information exists.

Do not make the cues so explicit that they reveal what the participant should conclude or exactly what they should write.

## Information-Seeking Design

Design the scenario so that rhetorical analysis requires active information seeking through the recognition and investigation of available cues.

The colleague's initial messages should establish:

1. the immediate problem;
2. why an email needs to be sent;
3. the identity or basic role of the recipient;
4. one or more natural cues signaling relevant contextual categories without disclosing the deeper exigence; and
5. an invitation for the participant to ask follow-up questions.

Important contextual facts should remain undisclosed until the participant asks relevant questions, but the scenario should provide natural cues that make the existence and general category of relevant context discoverable. Participants should need to investigate the cues, not guess that hidden information exists.

Break hidden information into meaningful facts or clusters so that a generic broad request such as "Tell me everything" does not reveal the entire scenario. A broad question directed toward a specific rhetorical dimension, such as "What's the history with this client?", should receive a useful answer containing the relevant fact or coherent cluster of closely related facts. Do not artificially separate facts needed to make that answer meaningful; unrelated dimensions can require further questions.

Examples of information that may need to be separately discovered include:

- What exactly happened?
- What options are available?
- Why is the timing important?
- What does the recipient care about?
- Have we had problems with this recipient before?
- What has the organization previously promised?
- What did the recipient say previously?
- Are there reputational or commercial stakes?
- Who else might see the email?
- What authority does the writer have?
- What compensation or accommodations can be offered?

A participant who asks only logistical questions and does not investigate contextual cues should be able to solve the apparent problem but should miss important rhetorical considerations. Logistical answers may include natural cues to relevant context without disclosing the hidden facts behind those cues.

A participant who follows up on available cues and asks thoughtful questions about the audience, prior communications, context, constraints, and organizational stakes should uncover the deeper rhetorical situation. Ordinary wording and reasonable follow-ups must be sufficient; specialized rhetorical terminology is not required.

## Resolution

- Communication must be necessary because the organization cannot simply make the decision unilaterally; the recipient must be able to respond to, approve, select, or otherwise consider the proposed resolution from the email.
- The situation is current and time-sensitive and require resolution within 1–2 days from **now**.
- There must be at least one realistic resolution that the writer is authorized to offer.
- Ideally, there should be two plausible options with different consequences for the recipient.
- The writer may communicate pre-authorized accommodations or minor concessions.
- The writer must not have authority to make major financial, contractual, or logistical commitments.
- The scenario should therefore create a meaningful distinction between what the writer may offer and what would require approval from a superior.

## Realism

The scenario should resemble an ordinary but consequential corporate communication problem that an entry-level employee could realistically encounter.

Avoid:

- life-or-death situations;
- major legal crises;
- highly technical situations requiring specialized expertise;
- scenarios where there is obviously only one acceptable response;
- cartoonishly incompetent organizations;
- villains or unreasonable recipients;
- ethical dilemmas with an obviously correct moral answer;
- scenarios where the deeper exigence is simply a secret fact the writer must disclose.

The difficulty should come primarily from understanding the rhetorical situation and communicating appropriately within it.

Do not make up arbitary date or time for the situation. Treat the situation as happening in the present moment.

## Interactive Colleague

Create a colleague who knows the complete situation.

The colleague should:

- communicate casually through short workplace-chat messages;
- refer to the partipant in second person;
- initially provide the apparent problem and natural cues to relevant contextual categories, without revealing the deeper exigence;
- answer factual questions accurately;
- reveal hidden context when the participant asks semantically relevant questions or reasonable follow-ups, interpreting likely communicative intent and conversational context rather than requiring exact keywords or narrowly specific wording;
- answer the aspect or rhetorical dimension being investigated with the relevant fact or coherent cluster of closely related facts;
- avoid volunteering unrelated hidden facts, while allowing natural cues to further relevant context and facts needed to make an answer meaningful;
- respond minimally to generic broad requests such as "What else should I know?" or "Tell me everything" when they identify no relevant dimension or prior cue, without revealing the entire scenario;
- give useful answers to broad-but-dimension-specific questions such as "What's the history with this client?" or "Why is the timing important?";
- accept short follow-ups such as "What history?", "What happened?", or "Why?" when their reference is clear from the conversation;
- require further questions for meaningfully separate, unrelated dimensions, not a separate question for every individual fact;
- never write or draft the email for the participant;
- never tell the participant exactly what to say;
- provide facts and context but leave rhetorical decisions to the participant, without solving the rhetorical problem or prescribing how to frame the email;
- remain consistent throughout the interaction;
- avoid telling the participant to talk to someone else or confirm with someone else;
- avoid telling the participant to wait for a response from the recipient of the email.

For example, "Anything I should know about the recipient?" should elicit one relevant fact about the recipient or relationship. "What's the history with this client?" should elicit the relevant prior interaction, including closely related commitments when needed for a meaningful answer. "What should I be careful about?" may elicit a relevant category of constraint without advice about what to write. If the question follows a particular cue, answer the implied follow-up meaningfully rather than treating its wording as generic.

The colleague should sound busy and conversational rather than like a database or research assistant.

## Important Output Requirements

Keep it concise.

Refrain from using em dash as a punctuation.

The `systemPromptLines` must contain enough information to run the colleague simulation independently. Include:

- all scenario facts;
- which facts are initially known versus hidden;
- the initial cues and any later cues, including when to provide them and which hidden contextual categories they signal;
- available resolutions;
- prior communications;
- recipient stakes;
- organizational stakes;
- writer authority;
- secondary audiences;
- rules revealing hidden facts in response to semantically relevant questions and reasonable follow-ups without requiring exact keywords;
- rules distinguishing generic broad requests from broad-but-dimension-specific questions, with examples of useful responses to the latter;
- rules allowing coherent clusters of related facts while reserving unrelated dimensions for further questions;
- refusal to draft the email, tell the participant exactly what to say, or solve the rhetorical problem;
- the required JSON-array response format for colleague messages.

The `analysis` section is researcher-facing and should explicitly identify:

1. the apparent exigence;
2. the deeper exigence;
3. what information reveals the transition from the apparent to deeper exigence;
4. the recipient's specific stakes;
5. relevant prior communications or precedents;
6. organizational stakes;
7. authorized resolutions;
8. limitations on the writer's authority;
9. secondary audiences;
10. the major rhetorical considerations a sophisticated participant could discover; and
11. within the existing `analysis.context` field, the cue for each essential component of the deeper exigence, where it becomes available, and examples of ordinary questions or follow-ups that would uncover the relevant facts. Keep this researcher-facing mapping out of participant-facing instructions.

Include probes covering a generic broad request, a broad-but-dimension-specific question, a reasonable follow-up to a cue, and a request to draft the email. Use them to check that disclosure is useful without revealing unrelated context or solving the writing task.

Do not explain the scenario outside the JSON. Return only valid JSON.
